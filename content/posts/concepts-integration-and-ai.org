As a Met's fan, I'm used to Keith Hernandez harping on "good fundies." I mean, as a teacher as well. That was the topic of Alfred Thompson's
[[https://blog.acthompson.net/2026/11/teach-concepts-not-tools.html
][latest post]]. In there he references [[https://bootstrapworld.org/ai-education-starts-with-the-math/][this]] post by Bootstrap World.
 
Of course, one of the problems is what do we mean by fundamentals.

Even before reading those posts, I was going to write on this, at least a bit. I recently listened to a podcast that featured Titus Winters of Adobe where he commented on CS education. A friend referred me to the podcast and asked for my thougths. I'll write those up soon but for now, one of Winters' points was that while everybody agrees that we must still teach the fundamentals, we can't seem to agree what those fundamentals shoudl be.

In his post, Alfred didn't specifically talk about any fundamentals but rather focussed on the imporance of teaching concepts, whatever they be, rather than tools.

Bootstrap World specifically talked about math fundamentals as they relate to understanding AI as well as where those fundamentals might be taught.

At Stuy, we always focussed on the fundmentals, or rather, what we considered the important fundamentals. It's why even in the age of AI, StuyCS will need relatively little reworking to remain relevant.

That said, it's not like I haven't seen tool teaching at Stuy. That was my biggest complaint when I railed against how drafting was taught.

When I was a student at Stuy, we did a year of drafting/mechanical drawing. It was sometimes a wonderful course and sometimes it was just robotically making "plates" all year. Depended on the teacher.

When they shifted to half by hand and half computer, they made some interesting choices. First they started using a non mainstream cad program. Then, even when it was severely out of date, they continued to use it - they said, that by using an out of date program, students couldn't easily aquire a copy to use at home, which could be seen as good or bad, and finally, they spent a lot of time on the specifics of the package - "today, we'll learn about the elipse menu."

That last one got me.

It struck me as spending English class learning about the file menu.

It was all about learning a tool which was why, even though there were some good teachers, trying to teach a good course and some students getting great value from them, oveall, it was the most disliked course at Stuy.

Brief disclaimer - this is all talking about drafting from when I took it in the 1980s through let's say 2010. They still teach drafting today and it could be a wonderful course, it could be a lousy one. I don't know so don't take my indictment of the past as criticism of the present. Whole knew set of teachers in a whole new department.

Of course teaching the tool was and is largely unneeded. When in college, we had to do some light design work as we were reworking some spaces. We ended up with a copy of Autocad - the industry standard at the time and not a particularly learner friendly program and figured it out. Neither I nor my partner had every been shown how to use it but we were overall pretty tech savvy and I had my by hand drafting experience from Stuy and we got the job done.

Unfortunately, so much teacher training is now rote and so too many schools "enocurage" teachers to use prefab materials which border on scripted. None of this bodes well for teaching the fundies.

This brings us to the Bootstrap World post. They talk about the importance of understanding math to understand AI. I agree with this 100% but even with the math fundies, I do think there's still value to exposing students to a specific tool. In the post, BSW, alludes to the graphing calculator but indeed teachers had to spend at least a bit of time on the specific tool. The trick is not to get lost and only teach the tool.

Sidebar - way back, a few years after the graphing calculator came into vogue for AP Calc, I decoded as an exercise to try to do an AP Calc A exam using only precalculus techniques and things I knew on the calculator, for example, if I recognized a problem needed Simpson's rule, since I knew how to plug numbers into the calculator and it new how to do Simpson's rule, I could use it to solve the question. Any question that I couldn't solve using those two approaches I left blank. I passed (I think it would have been a 3). So, no calculus required to pass at least that one specific exam.

I like a lot of what BSW said but unless you relate the math concepts to AI, I don't think the students will get the benefit.

It's not enough to teach basic probability. Unless you somehow relate probability to how LLMs generate text, the students won't understand more about AI. Basic stats are great, but unless you show how they can be worked into ML solutions, again, no benefit in understanding AI.

So, relating the math to the subject, in this case AI is also important. BSW's stance is that it makes more sense to integrate into existing classes. The claim is that students already take those existing classes and if you make an AI or CS elective, it's opt in only, you're adding a requirement, or students might take it at the expense of another class.

All true, but I, at least in part disagree.

If you've read this blog over the years you know I'm a fan of BSW's work, specically their work in Algebra classes. They've developed a curriculum where students use Racket (nee Scheme) to learn some CS while learning Algebra.

It works, but I think that Algebra is a unique beast. It's one of the easier required classes, at least if you're going by NY State standardized test results, it's not infrequently taught over 2 years instead of one giving more time, and Bootstrap World's material marries well with the existing Algebra content.

I don't know if this will be true for all subjects and for AI.

I do think that AI literacy shouldn't be limited to CS - as someone mentioned in a comment in one of the CS Ed communities "everyone says that we have to teach proper use of AI in CS to prevent cheating, why not put the teaching into English to prevent cheating there" but wether you're trying to add AI literacy or AI understanding, whenever you add something to a class, you're either squeezing in more material or you're taking something away.

That works in Boostrap World Algebra but I don't know if it universally does.

I've taught a number of topics that I call "proto AI" - applications that we can approach in any class where students have some programming background. Each of these topics give a good feel for how AI works even though I wouldn't call them AI. Each one also takes at least a week if not longer.

I can teach those topics because I worked them into my syllabus instead of other applications but it was a choice not to do something else. If you're integrating into another existing class that has, say a standardized exam, will you really be ably to find the extra time needed? I dont' know.

I don't have a good answer here.

Sure, if I was designing my own school and didn't have any state requirements, I'd be able to do it - I think we do far too much in a lot of subject areas and do it at too shallow a level. I'd like to do less more deeply which would allow for the types of applications that could also bring in AI literacy and even AI unterstanding but then, nobody's offerning me a school to run.

Anyway, just some pre Thanksgiving thoughts spurred on by those two blog posts a the top.
